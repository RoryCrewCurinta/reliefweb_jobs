Trendlines stories · Part one

Where the cuts bit

Between 2022 and 2025, international humanitarian funding fell by 30%. Job adverts on ReliefWeb fell by 64%. This is when the fall came, and which jobs, places and organisations it hit hardest.

Job adverts: ReliefWeb, to end Oct 2026. Funding: ALNAP, Global Humanitarian Assistance Report 2026.

Scroll ↓

1 · The totals

The same peak

In 2022 international humanitarian assistance reached US$47.5 billion, the highest ALNAP has recorded.

The same year ReliefWeb carried 52,965 job adverts, the most in a series that starts in 2011.

They had not always moved together. In 2020, the first Covid year, adverts fell 16% while funding held at about US$35.6 billion.

To compare money with adverts, both lines are set to 100 in 2022.

1 · The totals

Then the gap

By 2025 funding was down 30%, to US$33.3 billion.

Adverts were down 64%, to 19,254. That is fewer than in 2012.

Hiring fell about twice as far as the money.

2 · The timing

Adverts moved first

In 2023 funding was flat (down 0.3%) while adverts fell 12%. In 2024 funding fell 13% and adverts 22%. In 2025, 20% and 47%.

Some of the early fall is a surge unwinding. Adverts for Ukraine and Poland went from 295 in 2021 to 3,064 in 2022, and adverts for epidemic control fell by 33% in 2023.

Organisations stop hiring before they stop spending, because a frozen vacancy is the cheapest cut there is. That would make adverts an early signal. But this is three year-on-year changes, which is a pattern and not a tested lead indicator.

2 · The timing

Then a step down

Annual figures hide when 2025 happened. Through 2024, ReliefWeb averaged 3,040 adverts a month.

January 2025 had 2,328. The United States paused its foreign assistance on 20 January. February had 1,576, a third fewer in one month.

From February to November the monthly count stayed between 1,418 and 1,870. It stepped down and did not come back.

There's a noticeable dip in December 2025 (726). I checked it against the source. I can't tell the cause from this data.

3 · Job types

Staff posts fell furthest

Adverts for jobs fell 67% between 2022 and 2025. Adverts for consultancies fell 45%.

So consultancies grew from 15% of all adverts to 23%, without growing in number.

This fits organisations swapping payroll for short contracts when income is uncertain. It could also reflect how ReliefWeb is used: I don't know how organisations choose between posting here, on their own sites and on national job boards.

4 · Seniority

Junior roles went first

Adverts asking for 0–2 years of experience fell 73%. Those asking for 10 or more fell 55%.

The timing differs more than the totals. Entry-level adverts fell 18% in 2023 and 35% in 2024, before the US cuts. Senior adverts barely moved in those years (down 2% and 5%), then fell 52% in 2025.

The slow squeeze narrowed the entry routes for people starting humanitarian careers. The 2025 shock was the first to reach senior posts.

5 · Countries

Almost everywhere fell

These are the 20 countries with the most adverts in 2022, by where the job is based. Nineteen fell by half or more. Syria is the exception; more on Syria below.

Kenya fell least of the nineteen, by 51%. The dashed line is the fall across all adverts, 64%.

5 · Countries

Three headquarters countries near the top

Adverts based in the United States fell 78%, in the United Kingdom 77% and in France 83%. Switzerland, another headquarters country, fell a more modest 59%.

France's 2022 was unusually high (3,788 adverts, up from 1,881 in 2021). Measured from 2021 its fall is 66%. And adverts based in the US include domestic programmes such as refugee resettlement, not only head-office roles.

Head offices and home-country programmes were cut at least as hard as field operations. I can't separate the two from the location alone.

5 · Countries

One country went the other way

Adverts based in Syria rose from 869 in 2022 to 1,584 in 2025, up 82%. The first ten months of 2026 already have 1,527.

The Assad government fell in December 2024.

The timing fits organisations hiring to work in parts of Syria they could not reach before. I have not yet checked how many organisations account for the rise.

5 · Countries

Where funding rose, hiring didn't follow

ALNAP finds that funding fell in 2025 for 18 of the 20 largest recipients. The two exceptions were the Occupied Palestinian territory (oPt), up 15%, and Myanmar, up 11%.

Myanmar's adverts fell 11% that year (210 to 187), against 47% for all adverts. It held up far better than the rest.

oPt did not. Adverts there fell 56%, from 958 to 420, in the year its funding rose.

Small numbers, and a country's funding and its job adverts need not move in the same year.

6 · Who held up

Local organisations fell least

International NGOs post 67% of adverts, so they set the average: down 64%. UN and other international organisations fell 71%.

Locally based NGOs fell 24%, and regional NGOs based in the global South 36%.

The local figures are small (1,304 adverts in 2022, 990 in 2025). And much of the Red Cross and Red Crescent fall (81%) is a surge unwinding: its adverts more than doubled in 2022, from 1,404 to 3,258.

6 · What held up

Climate fell late, not little

Of the 36 topics with at least 300 adverts in 2022, coordination fell most (83%), then epidemic control (80%), refugees and asylum (78%) and cash and voucher assistance (78%).

Climate, environment and energy fell least (38%). But it held until 2024 (1,312 adverts in 2022, 1,416 in 2024) and then fell 42% in a single year.

By career category there is little to see: 7 of 9 fell between 64% and 70%. Information management is the exception, down 29%.

6 · What held up

One small topic grew

The last chart left out topics with fewer than 300 adverts in 2022. Of the 19 smaller ones with at least 50 adverts in either year, one grew: AI and machine learning, from 26 adverts to 53.

Remote and third-party monitoring held level (67 to 65), and urban response nearly so (63 to 57). Localisation fell 32%, half the rate for all adverts, so its share nearly doubled, from 0.28% to 0.53%.

Small did not mean safe. Multi-purpose cash fell 80%, from 66 adverts to 13.

These are a few dozen adverts a year, so one organisation's hiring round can move them. A further 10 topics with fewer than 50 adverts, such as interoperability (14 to 18), are too thin to chart.

Remote monitoring holding while field posts fell fits organisations pulling staff back and paying others to check on programmes.

Where it stands

It hasn't stopped

The first ten months of 2026 carried 13,306 adverts, 22% fewer than the same months of 2025.

ALNAP's two scenarios for public donor funding in 2026 are falls of 2% and 11%.

Either hiring is overshooting the money again, as it did on the way down, or the funding scenarios are optimistic. The 2026 funding figures will say which.

How far to trust this

  • Adverts are not hires. They count posts advertised on ReliefWeb, which is not all humanitarian hiring. Organisations post there unevenly, and some have moved recruitment to their own sites.
  • 2022 is a high base. It was the peak for both series and included the Ukraine surge, so every "since 2022" figure here is measured from the top.
  • The funding series is annual and short. ALNAP's 2026 report gives five years (2021 to 2025) in constant 2024 prices, and the 2025 figure is preliminary. Monthly adverts are being set against yearly money.
  • Funding for 2019 and 2020 is an estimate. ALNAP's published totals in constant prices start in 2021. For the two earlier years, its public-donor figures in 2024 prices are added to its private-funding figures, which are adjusted with the same deflator. Private giving is about a sixth of the total, so the effect is small: run on 2021 to 2025, the same method lands within 0.6% of the published totals.
  • Nothing here shows cause. The falls are consistent with the funding cuts biting where the text says. Organisations also differ in size, donors and country mix.
  • Topics are a language model's tags. Topic counts run a little low, so trends are safer than levels. The methodology has the checks.
